Hot Springs Safety: The Rules That Keep a Good Soak Safe

JM Jake Morrison ·9 min read ·30 min Easy

Hot springs feel like nature at its friendliest: warm water, steam, and a view. Most of the time, that is exactly what they are. But the same features that make a spring relaxing, heat, water, and remoteness, are also what make it risky when you ignore the rules. The good news is that nearly every hot springs accident is preventable, and the rules fit on one page. This guide covers them with the numbers behind each one, so you know what is a real risk and what is just folklore.

Read this before your next trip, not during it. Fifteen minutes of prep here is the difference between a great soak and a story you tell in the emergency room.

What You Need

A thermometer (a cheap waterproof one, ten dollars), drinking water, a nose clip if you plan to dunk, and a phone with signal or a buddy. That is the whole kit. Everything else in this guide is knowledge.

Step 1: Check the Temperature Before Any Part of You Goes In

The comfortable and generally safe range for soaking is roughly 98 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission sets 104 degrees as the maximum for hot tubs and advises keeping it at 100 for safety. Above 104, limit yourself to short sessions and get out at the first sign of dizziness or a pounding pulse. Developed commercial springs post their pool temperatures on a sign. Wild springs do not, which is why you carry a thermometer.

The danger at the top end is not discomfort, it is scalding. Undeveloped source pools can exceed 140 degrees, which scalds skin in seconds, and some geothermal pools reach temperatures near boiling. Never step into an unfamiliar pool to test it. Dip the thermometer first, and test downstream of the vent rather than right at the source, where the water is hottest. If there is no temperature posted and no one around who knows it, that pool is a look-don't-touch pool.

Step 2: Time Your Soak

Soak for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, then get out and cool down before going back in. That is the standard advice from spring operators and health authorities alike, and the physiology behind it is simple. Hot water raises your core temperature and dilates blood vessels, which drops blood pressure. Stay in too long and you get light-headed, and light-headedness in a pool is how people drown in water that is only knee-deep.

Older soakers should be stricter. In Japan, where hot spring bathing is a daily habit for millions, the winter months are the danger season: moving from a warm room into a cold dressing room and then into hot water causes sudden blood pressure swings. Japan's 2023 vital statistics counted 6,541 people aged 65 and older who died or nearly drowned in bathtubs between November and April, roughly three times the number of traffic fatalities in the same period. Experts there recommend keeping bath water below 41 degrees Celsius (about 106 Fahrenheit) and limiting soaks to 10 minutes for older adults. The lesson travels well: shorter soaks, and warm the changing area first when it is cold out.

Step 3: Hydrate, and Skip the Alcohol

Soaking makes you sweat, often without noticing, because the water hides it. Dehydration plus hot water plus blood pressure drop is the combination behind most fainting episodes at springs. Drink a full glass of water before you get in and keep a bottle at the edge of the pool. Drink water, not beer.

Alcohol deserves its own warning because it is the most common factor in hot springs accidents. It dilates blood vessels further, impairs judgment, and makes you stay in longer than you should. Soaking right after a heavy meal has a similar effect on blood pressure. Save the drinks for after the soak, when your body temperature is back to normal. The soak is the treat. The beer is the reward for surviving it.

Altitude makes all of this worse. Most western hot springs sit at 5,000 feet or higher, where the air is drier and your body loses water faster without noticing. At elevation, dehydration sneaks up sooner, and the blood pressure drop from hot water hits harder. If you drove up from sea level the same day, take it especially easy on the first soak: shorter session, more water, no alcohol at all. Your body is already working harder than usual just to breathe.

Step 4: Keep Your Head Above Water

This is the rule people laugh at until they hear the reason. Warm freshwater, including hot springs, can harbor Naegleria fowleri, a free-living amoeba that causes a brain infection which is almost always fatal. Cases are very rare, fewer than ten a year in the United States, but more than 97 percent of people who get it die. It infects through the nose, not by swallowing.

The CDC's advice is blunt and simple: keep your head above water in hot springs, and hold your nose or wear a nose clip if you jump into any warm, still freshwater. Do not dunk your head, do not do handstands, and do not let kids submerge their faces. It is a tiny habit that eliminates a rare but catastrophic risk.

Step 5: Know Who Should Sit Out

Some people should check with a doctor before soaking, and a few should skip it. Prolonged immersion above 102 degrees is generally advised against during pregnancy, because it can raise body temperature enough to risk hyperthermia. People with heart conditions, low blood pressure, or diabetes should ask first, since hot water raises heart rate and drops blood pressure at the same time.

Children need the closest supervision. Drowning is the leading cause of death for children aged 1 to 4, and the large majority of young child drowning deaths in pools and hot tubs happen at home or at private gatherings, not at public facilities. At a spring, kids should soak in the cooler pools, stay within arm's reach, and never be left alone near the water, even for a minute. Men, for the record, are about twice as likely as women to drown in hot tubs, a statistic that says more about risk-taking than about swimming ability.

Step 6: Wild Springs and Developed Springs Are Different Worlds

Developed springs have posted temperatures, lifeguards or staff nearby, maintained paths, and water that is tested. Wild springs have none of that, and the risks are different in kind, not just degree. In Yellowstone, more than 20 people have died from burns after entering or falling into hot springs, and the thin crust around geothermal pools can break underfoot. The water in some of those pools is near boiling. The park's rule is absolute: stay on the boardwalks, never touch the water.

That does not mean wild springs are off limits. It means they demand more homework. Before you drive out to one, check where are the hot springs along your route and whether the ones you are eyeing are developed, monitored, and currently open. Conditions change with seasons, landslides, and closures, and a spring that was safe last year may not be this year. Arrive with a plan, a thermometer, and a buddy. Never soak alone at a remote spring, because the nearest help may be an hour away.

Step 7: Winter Soaking Has Its Own Trap

Winter is the best season to soak and the riskiest, and the trap is the temperature swing. Cold air, hot water, cold air again: each transition jerks your blood pressure around. The Japanese data makes the point starkly. Those 6,541 bathtub deaths among older adults in a single winter season were not caused by the water being too hot. They were caused by the shock of moving between hot and cold.

The fix is boring and effective. Warm up the changing area before you undress. Enter the water slowly instead of jumping in. Get out slowly too, and sit on the edge for a minute before standing, especially if you feel light-headed. Dry off and dress warmly immediately after, and do not drive until you feel steady. The soak should end with you warm and relaxed, not dizzy and chilled.

The One-Page Checklist

Tape this to your trip plan. Thermometer in the bag. Temperature checked, 98 to 104 degrees. Soak 15 to 20 minutes, then cool down. Water bottle at the pool edge, no alcohol before or during. Head above water, nose clip if you dunk. Kids within arm's reach. Wild spring? Check it is open, go with a buddy, stay on marked paths.

None of this is meant to scare you off hot springs. Millions of people soak safely every year, and the health case for warm water is real. The rules exist so the soak stays what it should be: the most relaxing hour of your trip, not the most memorable for the wrong reason. Share the rules with everyone in your group before you arrive, especially anyone visiting a spring for the first time.
