Double Your Smartwatch Battery Life: 9 Settings That Actually Work

JM Jake Morrison ·9 min read ·45 min Easy

Battery life is the number one complaint smartwatch owners have, and the numbers explain why. A standard Apple Watch Series 11 is rated for about 18 hours, which means it dies if you forget one nightly charge. At the other end of the shelf, a Garmin Instinct 3 is rated for up to 24 days in smartwatch mode. Same category, wildly different endurance. But here is what most people miss: the gap between those extremes is not just hardware. A huge share of it is settings.

I have spent the last few weeks testing this on three different watches, and the pattern is consistent. A handful of settings, the always-on display chief among them, account for most of the drain. Change them and a watch that died every evening starts lasting two or three days. These nine changes are ranked by impact, biggest lever first. Work them in order and stop whenever the battery life feels right.

What You Need

Your watch, your phone with the companion app installed, and about 45 minutes. No purchases, no tools, no disassembly. Everything below is a settings change you can undo in seconds if you do not like it.

Step 1: Turn Off the Always-On Display

This is the single biggest lever on the list, and it is not close. Look at what the display costs on real models. The Garmin Venu 4 is rated for up to 12 days in smartwatch mode, but switch on the always-on display and that drops to about 4 days. Two thirds of the battery, gone to the screen. Samsung's Galaxy Watch Ultra 2 is rated for up to 80 hours with the always-on display off and 60 hours with it on, a 25 percent penalty on an 800mAh battery.

The fix takes ten seconds. On Apple Watch, open Settings, then Display and Brightness, then Always On, and switch it off. On Samsung and Pixel watches, it lives under Settings and then Display. You lose nothing that matters: raise-to-wake still lights the screen the instant you lift your wrist, and it works so fast now that most people stop noticing the difference within a day. If you truly love glancing at the time without moving, keep it, but know it is the most expensive setting on your watch.

Step 2: Tame Brightness and Screen Timeout

With always-on handled, the next screen cost is brightness. Most watches ship with brightness cranked higher than anyone needs indoors. Turn on auto-brightness (sometimes called adaptive brightness) so the watch dims itself in dark rooms, and manually drop the level one or two notches below maximum. On a bright AMOLED panel, the difference between 70 percent and 100 percent brightness is visible on the battery chart within a day.

Then shorten the screen timeout, the "wake duration" setting that decides how long the screen stays lit after you raise your wrist. The default is often 15 seconds or more. Drop it to the shortest option, usually 5 to 10 seconds. You will still have plenty of time to read a notification, and the screen, the hungriest component in the watch, spends far less of the day lit.

Step 3: Rethink the Cellular Connection

If your watch has LTE, that radio is the second hungriest component after the screen. Here is the thing: most people bought the cellular model "just in case" and then never leave their phone behind. If that is you, the radio is burning battery to maintain a connection you never use. On Apple Watch, open the Watch app on your phone, go to Cellular, and you can leave it on but know that every workout with the phone in your pocket is cheaper on battery when the watch routes through Bluetooth instead.

The practical middle ground is airplane-adjacent thinking. When you know the phone is within Bluetooth range, which is most of the day for most people, the watch barely touches LTE. The drain hits when you go phone-free: runs, errands, the gym. That is exactly when cellular earns its keep, so do not disable it permanently. Just be honest about whether you need it on a normal Tuesday. Many people find they can leave the phone at home far less often than they imagined, and the battery thanks them.

Step 4: Prune Your Notifications

Every notification lights the screen, fires the vibration motor, and wakes the processor. One buzz is nothing. Two hundred a day is a measurable drain. Open the companion app on your phone and go through the notification list app by app. Ask the same question for each: does this need to reach my wrist within seconds?

Keep calls, texts, and anything genuinely urgent. Cut social media likes, game alerts, news headlines, shopping deals, and every app that notifies you about its own existence. Most people cut their daily notification count by half or more in five minutes, and the watch gets quieter in the way that matters: fewer interruptions, longer battery, calmer wrist. This is one of those rare changes that improves two things at once.

Step 5: Audit Background Refresh and Widgets

Watch faces are not just pictures. Every complication on the face, the weather, your calendar, stock prices, the sunrise time, is a tiny app refreshing on a schedule. A face with six live complications costs meaningfully more battery than a face with two. Open your watch face settings and strip it down to what you actually glance at. Time, date, activity rings, maybe the weather. The stock ticker can live on your phone.

While you are in there, check background app refresh in the companion app. Apps you installed once and forgot, the meditation app from January, the golf app from one vacation, may still be refreshing in the background. Turn off background refresh for anything you do not use weekly. This is digital decluttering with a battery payoff.

Step 6: Pick a Simpler Watch Face

Related to step 5 but worth its own mention: animated faces cost power. The animated jellyfish, the live photo face, the face with a sweeping second hand that redraws sixty times a second, all of them keep the processor and display busier than a static face. The difference is small per hour but it compounds across a day.

You do not need an ugly face. Plenty of clean, attractive faces are static. Pick one you like that does not move, set it as your daily driver, and save the animated showpiece for occasions. Think of it like choosing LED bulbs over incandescent: same light, fraction of the energy.

Step 7: Use the Right GPS Mode for the Workout

GPS is the third big drain, and it matters most to runners and cyclists. Modern watches offer multiple satellite modes, and the most accurate one is the most expensive. On the Garmin Venu 4, standard GPS mode is rated for about 20 hours of tracking, while the more accurate multi-band mode drops that to roughly 17 to 19 hours. That is a 10 to 15 percent tax for precision you may not need.

The rule of thumb: use multi-band or dual-frequency GPS only where the sky is obstructed, downtown with tall buildings, dense forest, deep canyons. For open-road runs, park loops, and track workouts, standard GPS is plenty accurate and meaningfully cheaper. Check your watch's sport settings, most let you set the satellite mode per activity, so your trail runs can use the expensive mode while your road runs sip power.

Step 8: Dial Back Continuous Health Monitoring

The optical heart rate sensor works by flashing LEDs into your wrist many times per second, and "continuous" really means continuous. Most watches let you choose how often it samples. All-day continuous monitoring, continuous blood oxygen, and all-day stress tracking each add their own steady drain. Unless you are actively training for something, you probably do not need all three running around the clock.

The sweet spot for most people: heart rate set to continuous during workouts and periodic otherwise, blood oxygen set to manual or sleep-only, stress tracking on only if you actually look at the data. Be honest here. If you have never once opened the stress graph, turn the sensor off and reclaim the battery. Data you never look at is just expensive noise.

Step 9: Charge Smarter, Not More Often

This last one does not extend a single charge, it extends the battery's life over years, which matters because a smartwatch battery is not user-replaceable on most models. Lithium batteries wear fastest at the extremes: sitting at 100 percent for hours, or regularly draining to zero. The widely recommended habit is keeping the charge roughly between 20 and 80 percent for daily use.

In practice, that means unplugging when it hits the high 80s instead of leaving it on the charger overnight, and topping up before it dies completely. Many watches now have an optimized charging feature that learns your routine and holds the charge at 80 percent until just before you wake up. Turn that on if your watch offers it. A battery that still holds its rated capacity after two years is worth more than any single settings tweak.

Measure the Difference

Here is how to know it worked. Before you change anything, note what time the watch usually dies or what percentage is left at bedtime. Make your changes, then check again after three days. Most people see a 40 to 100 percent improvement from steps 1 through 4 alone, which is the difference between charging every night and charging every other night.

And if you ever need maximum endurance for a specific event, a long hike, a travel day, a marathon, remember that every watch has a power saver or low power mode that bundles most of these changes into one switch. The Apple Watch Ultra 4, for example, stretches from about 50 hours to 84 hours in Low Power Mode. Learn where that switch lives before you need it, not during.
