Windows 11 Features Worth Using Every Day

JM Jake Morrison ·7 min read ·20 min Easy

Windows 11 has been out for years, and most people use about ten percent of it. The rest sits there, finished and polished, waiting for someone to press the right key combination. Nobody reads release notes for fun, so the good stuff spreads by accident: a coworker snaps a window into a corner and you ask how they did that.

This is the tour I wish someone had given me on day one. Every feature below is already on your PC, free, and takes a couple of minutes to set up. Think of it as a tune-up for the way you work, the same way you would tune up the way a room works by moving the furniture. Small changes, daily payoff.

What You Need

Just your PC and twenty minutes. One feature, PowerToys Run, needs a free install from the Microsoft Store. Everything else is already there waiting.

Step 1: Snap Windows Into Layouts

Hover over the maximize button of any window and a grid appears: halves, thirds, quadrants. Pick a slot and the window jumps into it, then Windows offers your other open windows to fill the remaining slots. It is the fastest way to set up a research layout. Browser on the left, notes on the right, done in two clicks.

The keyboard version is even faster. Win + Z opens the layout picker, and Win plus the arrow keys throws windows into halves and corners. Snap groups take it one step further: hover over an app icon on the taskbar and Windows offers to restore the whole group you snapped earlier. Close everything for a meeting, then bring the entire layout back with one hover.

Step 2: Set Up Virtual Desktops

Press Win + Tab and click New desktop. You now have a second, empty workspace. Keep work on one, personal stuff on another, and switch with Ctrl + Win + left or right arrow. It is like having two desks you can swap between, except it costs nothing and works on a laptop in a coffee shop.

The trick that makes it stick: give each desktop its own wallpaper. Right-click a desktop thumbnail in Task View, choose a background, and pick something distinct. Your brain learns "blue wallpaper means work" faster than any label. Three desktops is the sweet spot for most people: work, personal, and the project you keep meaning to start.

Step 3: Run a Focus Session

Open the Clock app and click Focus sessions. Set a timer, pick a task from Microsoft To Do, and start. Windows silences notifications, shows a small timer widget, and can play background music through Spotify if you want it. When the timer ends, you get a break reminder. It is the Pomodoro technique with the setup work already done for you.

Pair it with Do not disturb on a schedule. Go to Settings, then System, then Notifications, and set Do not disturb to turn on automatically during your work hours. The point is not to never see notifications. It is to decide when you see them instead of letting every app decide for you.

Step 4: Turn On Clipboard History and Voice Typing

Press Win + V. If you have never done it, Windows asks to turn on clipboard history. Say yes. From now on, everything you copy is kept in a stack you can browse and paste from, including the thing you copied three copies ago. Pin the items you reuse constantly: your address, your email signature, the meeting link. Pinned items survive reboots.

While you are collecting keyboard shortcuts, try Win + H. A microphone appears and you can dictate into any text field. It is startlingly accurate now, handles punctuation commands like "comma" and "new line," and beats typing for first drafts, long messages, and anyone with tired wrists. Two shortcuts, and you will wonder how you worked without them.

Step 5: Install PowerToys Run

PowerToys is Microsoft's free toolkit of extras, and Run is the star. Install PowerToys from the Microsoft Store, press Alt + Space, and start typing. Apps, files, settings, calculations, unit conversions: it finds all of them in one box, faster than Start menu search. Type "25*18" and it answers 450. Type part of a setting name and it opens that setting directly.

PowerToys has other gems worth a look. FancyZones builds custom window grids beyond what Snap offers, for people with ultrawide monitors. Text Extractor pulls text out of any image on screen with Win + Shift + T, which ends arguments about "what did that screenshot say" forever. One install, several upgrades.

Step 6: Connect Your Phone With Phone Link

If you carry an Android phone, Phone Link turns your PC into its second screen. Texts, notifications, photos, and even full app mirroring appear on your desktop. Reply to messages without picking up the phone, drag photos straight into documents, take calls through your PC's speakers.

Setup takes five minutes. Open Phone Link on your PC, install Link to Windows on your phone, scan the code. iPhone owners get a smaller set of features, mostly calls and messages over Bluetooth, which is still worth the two-minute setup. Either way, the phone stays in your pocket during work hours, and that alone is half the productivity win.

Step 7: Switch On Live Captions

Search the Start menu for Live captions and turn it on. From then on, any audio playing on your PC gets real-time captions: videos, calls, podcasts, even that meeting where someone's microphone sounds like a drive-through speaker. It runs on device, so nothing is uploaded anywhere.

It is an accessibility feature first and a convenience feature second, but the convenience is real. Watch a tutorial at low volume in a shared room. Follow a call with a heavy accent. Skim a recorded meeting by reading instead of listening. Once it is on, you will leave it on.

Step 8: Let Storage Sense Clean Up After You

Go to Settings, then System, then Storage, and turn on Storage Sense. Configure it to run monthly: delete temporary files, empty the recycle bin after 30 days, and clear out downloads you have not touched in a while. It is the closest thing Windows has to a self-cleaning oven.

This is the boring entry on the list and arguably the most valuable. Disk-full slowdowns creep up over months, and a scheduled cleanup means you never think about it again. Set it once, in under a minute, and cross "mysteriously full drive" off your list of future problems.

Pick Two and Start There

None of these features changes your life on its own. Together, they change how the PC feels: less friction, fewer interruptions, less time spent managing windows instead of doing the work. Pick two, use them for a week, and add more when the first two feel automatic.

One habit multiplies all of this: learn one new shortcut a week. Win + D shows the desktop instantly, Win + L locks the PC when you stand up, Win + Shift + S grabs a screenshot of any region. Small keys, used daily, compound into the feeling that the computer works at your speed instead of its own. The machine you already own is more capable than the way you have been using it, and that is the cheapest upgrade in computing.
