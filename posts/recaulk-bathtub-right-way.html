Re-Caulk a Bathtub the Right Way

JM Jake Morrison ·7 min read ·2 hrs Moderate

Look at the line where your bathtub meets the wall. If the caulk there is cracked, peeling, or spotted with mold that will not scrub off, water is getting behind it. Every shower sends a little more moisture into the wall cavity, and that is how you end up with rot, mold inside the wall, and a repair bill with three zeros.

Re-caulking is a Saturday morning job: about two hours of actual work plus a day of curing. Most people do it wrong in exactly one way, and it is not the part you would expect.

The Mistake: Caulking Over the Old Caulk

New caulk does not bond to old caulk. It bonds to clean tub and clean tile. Every failed re-caulk job I have ever seen skipped the removal step or did it halfway. If you take one thing from this guide, take this: the removal is the job. The new bead is the victory lap.

Step 1: Strip Every Bit of the Old Caulk

Start with a utility knife or a dedicated caulk removal tool. Cut along both edges of the old bead, where it meets the tub and where it meets the wall, then pull the strip out. For stubborn sections, a caulk remover gel softens it: apply, wait the time on the label, scrape. A plastic putty knife or an old toothbrush handles the residue in textured grout lines.

Be thorough to the point of annoyance. Run your finger along the joint when you think you are done. Any rubbery bits left behind are bond-failures waiting to happen. This step takes the longest and matters the most.

Step 2: Clean, Then Dry Completely

Scrub the joint with a bathroom cleaner to remove soap scum, then wipe it down with rubbing alcohol on a rag. The alcohol does two things: it removes the last film of residue, and it evaporates fast, which helps with the critical part.

The joint must be bone dry before new caulk goes on. Not mostly dry. Bone dry. Caulk applied over moisture will not adhere, and trapped moisture breeds the exact mold you are trying to eliminate. After cleaning, leave the bathroom ventilated for several hours, or overnight if you can. Point a fan at the tub. Patience here pays off for years.

Step 3: Tape the Lines

Run painter's tape along both sides of the joint, one strip on the tub and one on the wall, leaving a narrow gap where the caulk will sit. About a quarter inch of exposed gap is right for most tubs. The tape is what gives you those crisp, professional edges, and it is the difference between a bead that looks DIY and one that looks hired-out.

Press the tape edges down firmly so caulk cannot seep underneath.

Step 4: Cut the Nozzle and Lay the Bead

Use 100% silicone caulk labeled for kitchen and bath. Not latex, not "siliconized" acrylic. Pure silicone stays flexible, resists mold, and lasts years longer. It is harder to tool and clean up, which is why people avoid it, but it is the right material.

Cut the nozzle at a 45-degree angle, with the opening slightly smaller than the gap you are filling. You can always cut more off; you cannot cut less. Hold the gun at 45 degrees and push the bead forward into the joint rather than dragging it behind you. Pushing forces caulk into the gap. Move at a steady pace: too fast leaves gaps, too slow piles it up.

Step 5: Tool It Once, Then Leave It Alone

Wet your finger with soapy water (or use a caulk finishing tool) and run it along the bead in one smooth, continuous pass to shape it. One pass. The great temptation is to keep fiddling, but every extra touch makes it worse. Silicone starts skinning over quickly, and touching it after that pulls and wrinkles the surface.

Immediately peel the painter's tape away, pulling at a sharp angle away from the bead, while the caulk is still wet. Then walk away. Do not touch it, do not shower, do not splash it. Most silicone needs 24 hours to cure fully, some need 48. Check the tube and honor it.

Keeping It Sealed

A good silicone bead lasts five to ten years. What kills it early is standing water and harsh cleaners. Squeegee or towel-dry the tub edges after showers if you are feeling diligent, ventilate the bathroom during and after showers, and clean the caulk line with non-abrasive cleaner. When it eventually fails, you will know exactly what to do, because you have done it right once already.
