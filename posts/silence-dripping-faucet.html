Silence a Dripping Faucet This Weekend

JM Jake Morrison ·6 min read ·30 min Easy

Drip. Drip. Drip. It is the most passive-aggressive sound in the house, and it is costing you real money. A faucet dripping once per second wastes over 3,000 gallons a year. That is not a typo. The fix, though, is one of the most satisfying repairs a homeowner can do: thirty minutes, a five-dollar part, and the drip is gone for years.

Almost every drip comes from the same cause. Inside the faucet, a washer or cartridge presses against a valve seat to stop the water. Over time that seal wears down, hardens, or gets nicked by mineral deposits, and water starts sneaking through. Your job is to replace the worn part. That is the whole repair.

Before You Touch Anything

First, shut off the water. Look under the sink for the shut-off valves on the hot and cold supply lines and turn both clockwise until they stop. No valves under the sink? Shut off the main water supply to the house instead. Then open the faucet to release pressure and drain the remaining water.

Second, plug the drain. Drop a rag or a sink stopper into the drain opening. Faucet screws are small, round, and magnetically attracted to drains. Every plumber has a story about this. Do not become one.

Third, take a photo of the faucet and note the brand. You will need it at the hardware store to match the replacement part, and photos beat memory every time.

Take It Apart

Most faucets disassemble the same way. Pop off the decorative cap on the handle (it usually pries off with a flat screwdriver or even a fingernail), remove the handle screw underneath, and lift the handle off. Under it you will find a packing nut or a retaining nut. Loosen it with an adjustable wrench, turning counterclockwise, and the stem or cartridge will lift out.

If you have a two-handle faucet, you are probably looking at a compression stem with a small rubber washer held on by a brass screw at the bottom. If you have a single-handle faucet, you are probably looking at a cartridge, a plastic or brass cylinder that pulls straight out. Ball and disc faucets have their own internals, but the principle is identical: remove the sealing part, replace it, reassemble.

Match the Part

Take the old washer, O-ring, or cartridge to the hardware store with you. Washer assortments are cheap and cover most sizes, but cartridges are brand-specific, so matching matters. The store staff can help if you bring the old part and your photo. While you are there, pick up some plumber's grease. A dab on O-rings and threads during reassembly extends the life of the repair.

Also inspect the valve seat, the surface the washer presses against inside the faucet body. Run your fingertip over it. If it feels rough or pitted, a valve seat wrench (a few dollars) lets you unscrew and replace it. A new washer on a damaged seat will start dripping again within weeks, so this check is worth the thirty seconds.

Reassemble and Test

Reassembly is disassembly in reverse. Seat the new washer or cartridge, snug the nuts back down, reattach the handle. Do not overtighten: snug is enough, and overtightening is how new washers get damaged on day one. Turn the water back on slowly, then test the faucet. Run it hot, run it cold, then shut it off and watch. Dry as a bone.

If it still drips, the usual culprit is the valve seat you skipped checking, or a cartridge that was not fully seated. Take it apart once more before you declare defeat. Nine times out of ten, the second look finds it.

When This Is Not Your Job

Two situations call for a plumber. If the shut-off valves under the sink are frozen or broken, forcing them can flood a cabinet, and that is a bigger job than a faucet. And if water is leaking from the base of the faucet or under the sink rather than dripping from the spout, the problem is a supply connection, not a washer, and it deserves professional attention.
