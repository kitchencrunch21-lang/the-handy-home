Build a Backyard Hot Springs Soak at Home

JM Jake Morrison ·9 min read ·1 weekend Moderate

A natural hot spring is hard to beat: mineral water, steam rising in cold air, and nothing to do but sit. The problem is that the nearest one might be a four-hour drive, and the good ones are crowded on weekends. A backyard soak fixes the commute. It will not be geothermal, but it can be the same ritual: hot water, quiet, and twenty minutes that belong to you.

The best part is the price. A new store-bought spa runs $5,000 to $15,000 installed, while most DIY soaking tubs land between $1,500 and $5,000 using upcycled materials, roughly 50 to 70 percent less. This guide walks through the whole project: picking a tub, preparing the pad, heating the water, keeping it clean, and staying safe. One weekend of work, years of soaks.

What You Need

A tub (stock tank, cedar kit, or concrete), a level pad, a heat source, a pump and filter kit if you want circulation, water care supplies, and a cover. Budget $1,500 to $2,700 for a complete stock tank setup, or $2,000 to $3,500 in materials for a cedar tub built from scratch. Tools are basic: level, shovel, drill, and a wrench set.

Step 1: Pick Your Tub

Three options cover almost every backyard. The stock tank tub is the cheapest and fastest: an 8 to 10 foot round livestock tank from a farm store runs about $600 to $900, and a complete plumbing kit with pump, heater, filter, controls, and jets runs $1,399 to $1,799. Total for tank and kit lands around $2,000 to $2,700, plus pipe, a base, a cover, and an electrician for the 240V supply if you go electric. Tanks come in galvanized metal or polyethylene plastic; plastic is lighter, will not rust, and the sloped sides are more comfortable to lean against.

The cedar tub is the prettiest option and the most hot-spring-like. Building one from scratch takes cedar planks, stainless steel bands, waterproof sealant, and a heater, with materials typically running $2,000 to $3,500. Cedar resists water damage naturally, and a wood-fired heater keeps the whole setup off the grid. A concrete tub is the permanent choice: most home builds are 6 to 8 feet wide and 3 to 4 feet deep, holding about 500 gallons, and will last for decades, but it is the most work and the most money. For a first project, the stock tank wins on every count except looks, and a cedar surround fixes that.

Step 2: Prepare the Pad

Water is heavy. A gallon weighs about 8.3 pounds, so a 500-gallon tub holds more than 4,000 pounds of water before you add people. It needs a level, solid base or it will settle unevenly and stress the tub. The cheapest good base is a leveled gravel pad topped with pavers, which runs about $100 to $200 and works well. A concrete pad is better for a permanent setup and must hold roughly 700 gallons of water for the larger builds.

Level the spot with a long level and a shovel, compact the gravel, and lay pavers tight. Leave 18 inches of clearance on at least two sides for access to the plumbing and heater. Plan drainage before you fill: the tub will need emptying for cleaning, and that water has to go somewhere that is not your foundation or your neighbor's yard. A gravel bed or a hose run to the lawn handles it.

Step 3: Choose Your Heat

Heating is the heart of the project, and there are three honest options. A wood-fired heater, like the Japanese Chofu stove that costs about $998, heats a 100-gallon tub with no electrical connection at all. It is slow, taking an hour or more to reach soaking temperature, and it needs tending, but it is silent, off-grid, and it turns heating the tub into part of the ritual. A 100-gallon fill costs about a dollar in water, so the operating cost is mostly your time and firewood.

An electric spa heater is the convenient option: set the temperature, walk away, and come back to hot water. It needs a 240V circuit installed by an electrician, and it will show up on the power bill, but it holds temperature automatically and pairs with a pump and filter. A solar setup, a coil of black pipe on a sunny roof, can heat a small tub for about $300 in parts, but it depends on weather and season. Most builders pick electric for daily use and wood-fired for the cabin feel. Either way, aim for the same target the pros use: 100 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit, never above 104.

Step 4: Plumb, Filter, and Circulate

Still water goes stale fast. A basic setup needs a pump to circulate water through a filter, and the complete kits make this a bolt-together job: pump, filter cartridge, heater, and controls arrive matched to each other. If you are building piecemeal, use Schedule 40 PVC as a minimum, Schedule 80 for hot water runs, and keep pipe runs short and insulated. Heat loss through bare pipe in winter is real money.

Place the pump and filter where you can reach them without climbing into the tub, and put unions, removable pipe joints, on both sides of every component so you can swap a pump or heater without cutting pipe. Add a drain valve at the lowest point of the system. You will thank yourself the first time you winterize it.

Step 5: Insulate and Make It Look Good

A stock tank looks like farm equipment until you dress it. The highest-impact upgrade is a cedar surround: frame the tank exterior with pressure-treated 2x4s, clad it with vertical cedar boards, and seal for weather resistance. A cedar surround runs $200 to $600 in materials and transforms the tub from utilitarian to intentional. It also hides hoses, the pump, and the metal shell.

Insulation matters as much as looks. Glue rigid foam board to the outside of the tank before cladding, and add a floating cover or insulated lid. A covered tub loses far less heat overnight, which means less reheating and a lower power bill. Add a non-slip step for entry, a small deck or gravel apron around the rim, and a privacy screen if the neighbors can see in. Four additions, one weekend, and it looks designed instead of improvised.

Step 6: Keep the Water Clean

Hot water grows things. Plan on about $20 a month in water care chemicals: a sanitizer, pH test strips, and a shock treatment. Test the water two or three times a week at first, until you learn how fast your setup drifts. Keep the pH in range, shock after heavy use, and change the water every few weeks depending on how often you soak. A filter cartridge needs rinsing weekly and replacing every few months.

Two habits prevent most problems. Shower before you soak, because lotions, sunscreen, and sweat are what cloud the water and feed bacteria. And keep the cover on whenever the tub is not in use. It keeps out leaves, bugs, and rain, holds the heat in, and keeps kids and pets from falling in, which is the safety point that matters most.

Step 7: Cover It and Lock It Down

Here is the sobering number: drowning is the leading cause of death for children aged 1 to 4, and the large majority of young child drowning deaths in hot tubs and pools happen at home. A locking cover is not optional, it is the project. Buy a cover rated for your tub's size, keep it locked whenever the tub is unattended, and fence the area if local code requires it. Many towns require a fence or locking cover for any outdoor water feature deeper than 18 inches, so check your local rules before you build.

For adults, the rules are simpler: soak 15 to 20 minutes at a time, get out and cool down before going back in, drink water instead of beer, and never soak alone if you have a heart condition or low blood pressure. Hot water drops blood pressure, and fainting in a tub is dangerous even in shallow water.

The Real Cost, All In

Add it up for the most common build, a stock tank with an electric kit: tank $600 to $900, plumbing kit $1,399 to $1,799, pad $100 to $200, cover $150 to $300, electrician a few hundred, and chemicals $20 a month. Call it roughly $2,500 to $3,500 all in, against $5,000 to $15,000 for a store-bought spa. The wood-fired version can come in near $1,500 total if you keep the build simple.

One more cost to budget: time. The first fill and heat-up takes an afternoon, and you will spend the first week fiddling with temperature and chemicals. After that, the routine settles to about fifteen minutes a week: test the water, skim the surface, wipe the waterline. That is less time than mowing the lawn, and the payoff is a soak waiting every evening.

That is the whole project: a weekend of work, a few thousand dollars at most, and a soak waiting in your backyard every evening. The nearest hot spring is now your back door.
